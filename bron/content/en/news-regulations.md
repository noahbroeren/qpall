## Why regulations matter when you choose pallets

When buying pallets, you choose between wood and plastic. That choice has long been about more than price. Companies increasingly look at sustainability, cost efficiency and environmental impact when they buy, as part of corporate social responsibility (CSR).

Legislators set requirements too, especially for packaging. Below you can read which rules matter most for plastic pallets and how we deal with them.

## The PPWR: European rules for packaging

For packaging, the most important European rule is the PPWR, the EU packaging regulation (Regulation (EU) 2025/40). It entered into force in February 2025 and has applied since 12 August 2026. Pallets are transport packaging, so the rules apply to your pallets as well.

The main points for pallets:

* **Reuse.** From 2030, at least 40% of transport packaging within the EU must be reusable.
* **Recyclability.** From 2030, packaging must be recyclable in grade A, B or C. Grade C means at least 70%.
* **Documentation.** The manufacturer draws up an EU declaration of conformity. For most of our models you can download it directly on the product page.

What this means for your pallets in detail is explained in our guide [PPWR and plastic pallets](@guide-ppwr).

## Heavy metals: RoHS and XRF analysis

The PPWR sets a limit for heavy metals in packaging. Lead, cadmium, mercury and hexavalent chromium together may not exceed 100 mg/kg.

We also use the substance list of the RoHS directive (Restriction of Hazardous Substances) as a benchmark. That directive was written for electronics, not for packaging, but its limit values are a strict test for recycled plastic. RoHS restricts substances including:

* lead (Pb), mercury (Hg) and cadmium (Cd)
* hexavalent chromium (CrVI)
* polybrominated biphenyls (PBB) and polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDE)

### How does XRF work?

X-ray fluorescence (XRF) is a fast method for measuring elements in materials such as plastic, metal or soil. The material stays intact. XRF measures the total amount of an element, down to ppm level (parts per million). That way we can see straight away whether lead, cadmium, mercury, chromium or bromine is getting close to a limit value. Determining hexavalent chromium or certain bromine compounds separately requires a laboratory analysis.

This shows us whether our pallets stay within the limits. That check is especially important for recycled plastic, because the material comes from different waste streams.

## ISPM 15: not for plastic pallets

ISPM 15 is an international phytosanitary standard. It protects plant health: insects and plant diseases must not cross borders in wooden packaging. Wooden pallets for export must therefore be treated, usually with heat, and carry an official stamp.

Plastic pallets are not covered by ISPM 15. You can use them without treatment or a stamp, including for [export](@type-export) outside the EU. Read more in [ISPM 15: export without a stamp](@guide-ispm).

## CSR and the carbon footprint

Not everything is a matter of law. Many companies set their own purchasing requirements as part of CSR. The carbon footprint is a widely used yardstick here. It shows the greenhouse gas emissions needed to make a pallet, expressed in kilograms of CO₂ equivalent (kg CO₂e). Most emissions come from extracting raw materials, transport and production.

For customers who compare products, the carbon footprint is increasingly a deciding factor, alongside price and quality.
